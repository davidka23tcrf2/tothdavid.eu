    How this works

    Every non-zero number's first significant digit is counted (0.0071 counts as a 7). Benford's law says digit d should lead with probability log10(1 + 1/d).

    The second digit and first two digits tests follow from the same law and are the next steps in a forensic audit: the first-two test in particular picks out single amounts that were used too often. The last two digits test isn't Benford at all. For numbers with three or more digits the endings should be spread evenly, so a pile-up on 00, 50 or 99 usually means rounding, estimates or invention.

    Correlation is Pearson's r between your digit shares and Benford's. MAD is the average gap per digit, judged with Nigrini's thresholds (under 0.006 close, 0.012 acceptable, 0.015 marginal, above that nonconforming). Chi-square asks whether the gaps are bigger than chance, but with thousands of rows it flags even harmless wobbles, which is why the headline number doesn't lean on it.

    The headline number is the most suspicious of the four tests that had enough numbers, because each test only sees part of the picture: someone who invents amounts with random later digits passes the second-digit and last-two tests but fails the first-digit one, and someone who copies real leading digits but rounds everything fails the last-two test.

    Each test's estimate is a Bayesian comparison of two explanations: “real data, Benford-shaped with the usual real-world wobble” versus “a noticeably different pattern, like the ones people produce when they make numbers up”. It starts at 50/50 and updates on your counts, so small samples stay near the middle and large ones become decisive.

    A bad fit isn't proof of fraud. Benford only applies to data that spans several orders of magnitude and isn't capped or assigned: invoice totals, populations, river lengths, stock volumes. Ages, prices ending in .99, phone numbers and lottery draws won't fit and aren't meant to.
